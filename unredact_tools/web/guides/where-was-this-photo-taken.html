What gives a location away

  • GPS coordinates in the photo's EXIF data. If they're there, the scan shows them with a map link. They're usually accurate to a few meters.
  • Altitude and compass direction, which show roughly how high up the camera was and which way it was pointing.
  • The timezone offset saved with the capture time, which narrows down the part of the world even without GPS.
  • The thumbnail of the original. A cropped photo can still carry a small preview of the full, uncropped shot.

Clues in the picture itself

Even with every bit of metadata removed, the image can be enough. Street signs, shop names, house numbers, license plates, landmarks, the view from a window, and reflections in glasses or mirrors are all used to locate photos. Shadows matter too: with a known time, the direction and length of shadows narrows down where on Earth a photo could have been taken.

The scan reads text in the image, finds license plates, faces and QR codes, and for GPS-tagged photos shows where the sun was at capture.

Before you share a photo

  • Check it with the scanner above and clean it. The clean copy has no GPS, no timestamps and no thumbnail.
  • Black out text and plates, and blur faces of people who didn't agree to be in it.
  • Look at the background the way a stranger would. If you'd recognize the place, so might they.
