What the dots are

Many color laser printers print a faint pattern of yellow dots across every page. It's known as a Machine Identification Code. The dots are small and yellow on white, so you won't see them without magnification or a blue light, but a good scan picks them up.

Depending on the printer, the pattern can encode the printer's serial number and the date and time the page was printed. It repeats across the page, so even a small piece of a printout can carry it.

Why it matters

If you print a document and then scan or photograph the printout to share it, the dots come along. Anyone with the scan and the right tools can read them, and the printer's maker can link a serial number to where the printer was sold or registered. In 2017, dots in a published scan of a leaked NSA document showed the printer's serial number and the time it printed. More cases like this.

What the scan does

  • Isolates faint yellow specks in images and in the pages of scanned PDFs.
  • Looks for the regular grid that tracking codes use.
  • Decodes the Xerox DocuColor pattern, one of the best-documented codes, into a serial number and print time.
  • Removes the dots when you clean the file. You can keep them with the Keep printer dots option if you need the scan unaltered.

Not every printer's code has been decoded publicly, so the scan may find a dot pattern without being able to read it. Finding one is enough reason to clean the file.

Other ways to avoid them

  • Share the original digital file, cleaned, instead of a printout.
  • If you must share a printout, a black-and-white copy or retyped text avoids the yellow dots, though the text itself can still be traced if copies were marked.
